Is Immigrating to Canada Still Worth It in 2026? My Honest 8-Year Review

✍️ Anuj Vaghela 📅 May 2026 ⏱ 15 min read

This question is likely keeping you up at night. After 8.5 years of building a life here — moving to Ontario, Canada in 2017 on temporary status — I'm ready to give you the truth.

This isn't a government brochure or a consultant's sales pitch. These are my personal observations, shaped by nearly a decade of navigating the Canadian immigration system as a student, worker, and permanent resident.

Packing your entire life into two suitcases is a massive gamble. To help you decide if it's the right one for you in 2026, I've broken down the "Canadian Dream" into 16 real-world categories.

A note on the comparison

My goal is to compare life in Canada to other countries for providing clarity and not to demean any country. I've spent a significant chunk of my life in both, and I know that "better" is subjective. What follows is perspective — the kind you only get after 3,000+ days on the ground.

💡 I will avoid overwhelming you with government statistics. Instead, I'll focus on lived experience — the stuff that only shows up after you're actually here, paying rent, filing taxes, and trying to build a life.

1. Is a Canadian Education Worth It for Immigrants?

Before you enroll in a Canadian college or university program, let's talk numbers honestly. A 4-year engineering degree at a Canadian university can cost international students over $200,000 — while the typical starting salary in that field lands between $60k and $80k. The math is challenging even before you factor in the difficulty of working part-time through an intense university program.

A 3-year college diploma in the same field, by contrast, might run around $60,000. With co-op placements built into the program, students can offset a significant portion of that cost through paid work. In my own case, more than 60% of my diploma was self-funded through co-op and part-time employment during the program itself.

The bigger question is: will the program you're considering actually make you employable in Canada? That's the only metric that matters for an immigrant making this investment.

2. The Reality of Finding Your First Job in Canada

Some newcomers arrive and have a job within two weeks. Others spend three months sending applications into what feels like a void. The difference usually isn't qualifications — it's approach.

The biggest mistake I see newcomers make is staying home and submitting applications online. Canada's labour market, especially at the entry level, rewards visibility. Physically walking into businesses, carrying resumes, and asking to speak to a manager still works in retail, food service, and warehousing. Being physically present gives you something an online application never can: a face, a personality, a moment of actual connection.

The emotional reality of starting over is also worth naming. You may have been highly capable in your home country — a professional, a manager, someone with status. Here, you're starting from scratch. That adjustment takes time, and it's harder than most immigration guides will tell you.

3. The "Survival" vs. "Field" Job Gap

Most immigrants arrive with a plan: work a survival job for a few months, gain some Canadian experience, then transition into their actual career. For many, those "few months" quietly become two years. Then three. Then suddenly they're watching others advance professionally while still stacking shelves.

This gap — between the survival job that pays the rent and the field job that builds a career — is one of the most underreported realities of immigration in Canada. It's caused by three compounding barriers: no Canadian work experience in your field, foreign credentials being undervalued, and no professional network in this country. These barriers reinforce each other in a loop that's hard to break without a clear strategy.

The immigrants who cross the gap fastest are the most intentional, not the most talented. They network while studying. They apply for co-ops early. They treat their survival job as a financial support system, not an identity.

4. State of the 2026 Job Market

Canada's job market in 2026 is more competitive than it was in 2018 or 2019 — that much is simply true. Immigration levels increased dramatically over the past several years, which means there are more qualified candidates for entry-level and mid-level roles than there used to be. At the same time, some sectors are genuinely starved for skilled workers, particularly in trades, healthcare support, and certain technology niches.

The "Canada is desperate for workers" narrative that many immigrants heard before coming here was partially true and partially marketing. The reality is sector-specific. If you're a registered nurse, a licensed electrician, or a senior software developer with specialized skills, the market is very much open to you. If you're an internationally-trained lawyer, journalist, or generalist business professional, the path is longer and harder.

Understanding which category your field falls into before you arrive — rather than after — changes your entire strategy.

5. Canadian Workplace Culture

Canadian workplace culture is polite in ways that can feel opaque to someone new. Feedback is rarely direct. Disagreements are softened with so many qualifiers that you might leave a difficult meeting unsure whether anything was actually said. There is a strong culture of consensus-building, which can feel inefficient if you come from a more hierarchical work environment.

What surprised me most was the emphasis on psychological safety — or at least the language around it. Managers in Canada are generally not expected to be authoritarian, and explicit micro-management is genuinely frowned upon in most professional environments. The trade-off is that accountability can feel vague, and it takes time to read the unspoken social codes.

For immigrants, the adjustment is less about technical skills and more about communication style. Learning to give and receive feedback the Canadian way — indirect, affirming, wrapped in politeness — is one of the most important soft skills you can develop early.

6. Earning Potential: Expectation vs. Reality

Most people who immigrate to Canada are aware that salaries are higher in nominal terms than in their home country. What many don't fully account for is how much of that salary disappears before it becomes purchasing power. Provincial income tax, CPP contributions, and EI premiums can claim 25–35% of a mid-level salary. Add rent, utilities, and groceries, and what looked like a generous income on paper can feel surprisingly tight in practice.

In fields like engineering technology, nursing, and skilled trades, experienced workers in Ontario or BC can realistically reach $70,000–$100,000+ within five to eight years of arrival — if they transition successfully from survival jobs to field jobs. The key phrase is "if they transition successfully." That transition is far from automatic.

The immigrants I've watched build financial security here all shared one trait: they were aggressive about the field-job transition in their first three to four years, rather than settling into the comfort of a steady survival job paycheck.

7. The True Cost of Living

Canada is expensive. That's not a controversial statement anymore — even Canadians say it openly. But the nature of that expense is worth understanding in detail, because it's unevenly distributed across categories in ways that affect immigrants differently than long-term residents.

Groceries have risen sharply. A weekly grocery run for a single person in Ontario can easily run $100–$150 if you're buying anything beyond the absolute basics. Rent in major cities has become genuinely prohibitive for newcomers at survival-job wages. A one-bedroom apartment in Toronto starts around $2,200 per month. Even in mid-sized cities like London or Hamilton, that same apartment is $1,600–$1,900.

The saving grace is that healthcare is covered once you're a resident, and public transit in larger cities is functional even if not exceptional. But if you're running the numbers on whether Canada is affordable on a $17–$19/hour survival job wage, the honest answer is: it's survivable, but not comfortable, and probably not sustainable long-term without a clear path to higher earnings.

8. Surviving the Chilly Winters

If you've never experienced a Canadian winter, the first one will reset your understanding of cold. In Ontario, temperatures routinely drop to -15°C to -25°C with wind chill between December and February. In the Prairie provinces, -30°C is not unusual. The cold is a physical and psychological adjustment that no amount of YouTube videos fully prepares you for.

The practical realities: your wardrobe investment in the first year will be significant. A proper winter coat, boots, gloves, thermals, and accessories can easily cost $400–$700 if you're buying quality gear that will actually keep you functional. Heating bills spike dramatically in winter months. Your commute becomes longer and harder. Driving on ice is a skill that takes a full season to develop, and the anxiety of it is real.

What most immigrants eventually tell me is that the first winter is the worst, the second is manageable, and by the third you've developed a kind of grim acceptance that becomes almost pride. Winter is a shared suffering that bonds Canadians in a uniquely Canadian way — and eventually, it will bond you too.

9. The Rent Crisis: What $2,500 Gets You

$2,500 per month used to get you a comfortable two-bedroom apartment in most Ontario cities outside Toronto. In 2026, that same budget rents you a modest one-bedroom in Toronto, a decent one-bedroom in Hamilton or Kitchener, or a two-bedroom in a smaller city like Guelph or Barrie.

The calculus has shifted significantly for newcomers. Roommates are no longer just an option — for many, they're a financial necessity during the survival job phase. Sharing a three-bedroom apartment between three people in the $2,000–$2,200/month range keeps individual costs manageable enough to save while building toward something. It's not glamorous, but it's the math that works.

What I tell every newcomer asking about housing: budget $800–$1,000 per person per month for rent in your first year if you're sharing, and treat anything below that as a bonus. Plan your first 12 months around that number, and adjust as your income grows.

10. Getting Around: Transportation

Canada is a car-dependent country outside of its major city cores. If you're living in Toronto or Vancouver and working near transit, you can genuinely function without a vehicle. If you're anywhere else — the suburbs, a mid-sized city, a smaller town — a car is essentially mandatory within the first year or two.

Getting a Canadian driver's licence as a newcomer takes time. Most provinces require a graduated licensing process that can take 12–18 months to complete from scratch, even if you have years of driving experience in another country. Some countries have reciprocal agreements with Canadian provinces that allow licence exchanges, but many don't — which means new immigrants are starting from the beginning.

The good news: public transit in cities like Toronto, Ottawa, and Calgary is functional for daily commuting, and many employers in city centres are accessible by transit. The honest picture is that transit in Canada ranges from excellent to barely adequate depending entirely on where you live, and planning your housing with transit access in mind is one of the most practically important decisions a newcomer can make.

11. Food: The Taste of Home vs. Grocery Bills

One of the things that genuinely surprised me about Canada is how accessible South Asian, East Asian, and Caribbean food is in most major urban centres. You can find decent roti in Brampton, reasonable biryani in Mississauga, and surprisingly good East African food in certain Toronto neighbourhoods. The multicultural food landscape is one of Canada's genuine strengths, and it softens the homesickness in a tangible way.

The cost is the challenge. Specialty imported items — specific spices, lentil varieties, certain vegetables — often cost two to four times what you'd pay back home. Meat prices are high across the board. And if you're trying to eat well on a survival-job budget, the math gets difficult quickly.

The strategy that works for most newcomers: learn the South Asian grocery stores (Oceans Fresh Food Market and similar chains are far cheaper than regular supermarkets for staples), buy in bulk where possible, and accept that your food costs will be higher here than you're used to. Eating well in Canada on a budget is possible, but it requires more intentional shopping than most people are used to.

12. The Hidden Mental Toll of Immigration

No one talks about this enough. The immigration experience, even a successful one, involves a type of grief that doesn't have a clean name. You grieve the life you left. You grieve the version of yourself that was known and understood. You carry a constant, low-grade awareness that your family is on the other side of a very long flight, and that major events in their lives — illnesses, milestones, deaths — will often happen without you physically present.

Add to that the professional demotion of the survival job phase, the social isolation of building a network from scratch, the cognitive load of navigating an unfamiliar system, and the pressure of making the immigration decision "worth it" — and you have a mental health challenge that no one warned you about in the application packet.

What has helped the people I've watched navigate this successfully: staying connected to community (religious, cultural, or simply finding people who understand the experience), having clear progress markers so the hard season feels temporary, and giving themselves permission to not be fine all the time. The resilience required to immigrate is real. Acknowledging that it costs something is not weakness — it's accuracy.

13. Navigating New 2026 Permit Rules

Canada's immigration policy landscape shifted noticeably between 2024 and 2026. International student caps were introduced. The pathways for international graduates to transition from study permits to post-graduation work permits became more competitive. Certain streams that previously had high acceptance rates became significantly more selective as Canada adjusted immigration levels in response to housing and labour market pressures.

For anyone currently on a temporary permit — study permit, PGWP, open work permit — the single most important thing to understand is the transition timeline. Many newcomers underestimate how little buffer they have between the end of their current permit and the need for their next one. Processing times are longer than the official targets suggest, and living in status limbo while waiting for a decision is genuinely stressful.

The safest approach in 2026 is to start thinking about your next permit status at least six months before your current one expires, work with a reputable regulated consultant (RCIC) for anything complex, and avoid relying on information from Facebook groups or YouTube channels that may be months out of date.

14. The Long PR Journey

Permanent residency is the milestone that changes everything. With PR, you have access to healthcare, most social programs, the ability to work for any employer, and the right to live anywhere in Canada. It also removes the anxiety of permit renewals and the vulnerability that comes with temporary status.

The Express Entry system remains the most well-defined path to PR for skilled workers, but competition has intensified. The Canadian Experience Class (CEC) — which prioritizes candidates who already have Canadian work experience — rewards immigrants who got into the field job phase and built up their CRS score. A score around 460+ is typically competitive, though the cutoff fluctuates with each draw.

The most underrated PR strategy I've seen work consistently: arrive with qualifications, get Canadian credentials that qualify you for Express Entry, transition to a field job, and let your score build while you're working. The people who PR fastest are usually the ones who didn't try to shortcut the process — they worked the system exactly as it was designed.

15. An Honest Talk About Racism

Canada has a reputation as a multicultural, tolerant society — and in many ways, that reputation is earned. Explicit, aggressive racism in professional settings is genuinely less common here than in many countries. But the absence of overt racism doesn't mean the absence of racism. What immigrants more commonly encounter is systemic and subtle: the resume that doesn't get a callback because the name reads as South Asian or East African, the extra scrutiny in professional settings, the assumptions about language ability or cultural fit.

The experience varies significantly by city, industry, and individual. Brampton is a different experience than a small town in Saskatchewan. Tech companies in Toronto tend to be more diverse than law firms in Bay Street. And frankly, some of it depends on how you present yourself, how you communicate, and how quickly you learn to navigate the cultural codes.

My honest assessment after 8.5 years: Canada is genuinely more liveable for immigrants than many countries I could name. It is also not the post-racial utopia that some immigration consultants imply. You will experience moments of being othered. You will navigate workplaces where the implicit expectations were built around people who don't look or sound like you. Knowing that going in — and developing the resilience to not let it define your experience — is part of what makes the long-term story here a good one.

16. Decision-Making Questionnaire

After reading through all of this, you may still be sitting with the original question: is it worth it? The honest answer is that it depends — on your field, your financial runway, your family situation, your personality, and your tolerance for a hard first few years in exchange for something more stable afterward.

To help you think through it clearly, I'm building a decision-making questionnaire that walks through your specific situation: your CRS score, your field's demand in Canada, your financial runway, your family obligations, and your personal risk tolerance. The goal is not to make the decision for you, but to make sure you've actually thought through the factors that matter most.

If I had to distill it to one framework: Canada rewards immigrants who are willing to start lower than their qualifications suggest, build systematically, and stay patient for three to five years. If that describes you — and if your field is one where the Canadian market genuinely has demand — the long-term case is still strong in 2026.

Final thoughts

The Canada of 2026 is not the Canada of 2018. The rules have changed, the costs have risen, and the competition for permits and jobs is sharper. But the fundamentals that made Canada worth it for me — a functional immigration pathway, a professional environment that values skill, a genuinely multicultural urban fabric, and the stability that comes with permanent residency — are still intact.
