Is Immigrating to Canada Still Worth It in 2026? My Honest 8-Year Review

✍️ Anuj Vaghela 📅 May 2026 ⏱ 15 min read

This question is likely keeping you up at night. After 8.5 years of building a life here, moving to Ontario, Canada in 2017 on temporary status, I'm ready to give you the truth.

This isn't a government brochure or a consultant's sales pitch. These are my personal observations, shaped by nearly a decade of navigating the Canadian immigration system as a student, worker, and permanent resident.

Packing your entire life into two suitcases is a massive gamble. To help you decide if it's the right one for you in 2026, I've broken down the "Canadian Dream" into 16 real-world categories.

A note on the comparison

My goal is to compare life in Canada to other countries for providing clarity and not to demean any country. I've spent a significant chunk of my life in both, and I know that "better" is subjective. What follows is perspective, the kind you only get after 3,000+ days on the ground.

💡 I will avoid overwhelming you with government statistics. Instead, I'll focus on lived experience. That is the stuff that only shows up after you're actually here. Paying rent, filing taxes, and trying to build a life.

1. Is a Canadian Education Worth It for Immigrants?

Before you enroll in a Canadian college or university program, let's talk numbers honestly. A 4-year engineering degree at a Canadian university can cost international students over $200,000 — while the typical starting salary in that field lands between $60k and $80k. The math is challenging even before you factor in the difficulty of working part-time through an intense university program.

4. State of the 2026 Job Market

Canada's job market in 2026 is more competitive than it was in 2018 or 2019 — that much is simply true. Immigration levels increased dramatically over the past several years, which means there are more qualified candidates for entry-level and mid-level roles than there used to be. At the same time, some sectors are genuinely starved for skilled workers, particularly in trades, healthcare support, and certain technology niches.

5. Canadian Workplace Culture

Canadian workplace culture is polite in ways that can feel opaque to someone new. Feedback is rarely direct. Disagreements are softened with so many qualifiers that you might leave a difficult meeting unsure whether anything was actually said. There is a strong culture of consensus-building, which can feel inefficient if you come from a more hierarchical work environment.

6. Earning Potential: Expectation vs. Reality

Most people who immigrate to Canada are aware that salaries are higher in nominal terms than in their home country. What many don't fully account for is how much of that salary disappears before it becomes purchasing power. Provincial income tax, CPP contributions, and EI premiums can claim 25–35% of a mid-level salary. Add rent, utilities, and groceries, and what looked like a generous income on paper can feel surprisingly tight in practice.

8. Surviving the Chilly Winters

If you've never experienced a Canadian winter, the first one will reset your understanding of cold. In Ontario, temperatures routinely drop to -15°C to -25°C with wind chill between December and February. In the Prairie provinces, -30°C is not unusual. The cold is a physical and psychological adjustment that no amount of YouTube videos fully prepares you for.

9. The Rent Crisis: What $2,500 Gets You

$2,500 per month used to get you a comfortable two-bedroom apartment in most Ontario cities outside Toronto. In 2026, that same budget rents you a modest one-bedroom in Toronto, a decent one-bedroom in Hamilton or Kitchener, or a two-bedroom in a smaller city like Guelph or Barrie.

10. Getting Around: Transportation

Canada is a car-dependent country outside of its major city cores. If you're living in Toronto or Vancouver and working near transit, you can genuinely function without a vehicle. If you're anywhere else — the suburbs, a mid-sized city, a smaller town — a car is essentially mandatory within the first year or two.

11. Food: The Taste of Home vs. Grocery Bills

One of the things that genuinely surprised me about Canada is how accessible South Asian, East Asian, and Caribbean food is in most major urban centres. You can find decent roti in Brampton, reasonable biryani in Mississauga, and surprisingly good East African food in certain Toronto neighbourhoods. The multicultural food landscape is one of Canada's genuine strengths, and it softens the homesickness in a tangible way.

12. The Hidden Mental Toll of Immigration

No one talks about this enough. The immigration experience, even a successful one, involves a type of grief that doesn't have a clean name. You grieve the life you left. You grieve the version of yourself that was known and understood. You carry a constant, low-grade awareness that your family is on the other side of a very long flight, and that major events in their lives — illnesses, milestones, deaths — will often happen without you physically present.

13. Navigating New 2026 Permit Rules

Canada's immigration policy landscape shifted noticeably between 2024 and 2026. International student caps were introduced. The pathways for international graduates to transition from study permits to post-graduation work permits became more competitive. Certain streams that previously had high acceptance rates became significantly more selective as Canada adjusted immigration levels in response to housing and labour market pressures.

14. The Long PR Journey

Permanent residency is the milestone that changes everything. With PR, you have access to healthcare, most social programs, the ability to work for any employer, and the right to live anywhere in Canada. It also removes the anxiety of permit renewals and the vulnerability that comes with temporary status.

15. An Honest Talk About Racism

Canada has a reputation as a multicultural, tolerant society — and in many ways, that reputation is earned. Explicit, aggressive racism in professional settings is genuinely less common here than in many countries. But the absence of overt racism doesn't mean the absence of racism. What immigrants more commonly encounter is systemic and subtle: the resume that doesn't get a callback because the name reads as South Asian or East African, the extra scrutiny in professional settings, the assumptions about language ability or cultural fit.

16. Decision-Making Questionnaire

After reading through all of this, you may still be sitting with the original question: is it worth it? The honest answer is that it depends — on your field, your financial runway, your family situation, your personality, and your tolerance for a hard first few years in exchange for something more stable afterward.

Final thoughts

The Canada of 2026 is not the Canada of 2018. The rules have changed, the costs have risen, and the competition for permits and jobs is sharper. But the fundamentals that made Canada worth it for me — a functional immigration pathway, a professional environment that values skill, a genuinely multicultural urban fabric, and the stability that comes with permanent residency — are still intact.
